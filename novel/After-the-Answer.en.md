## One · The Cake

Vika came back from ancient Egypt with a shoe and missed the birthday of Sami's daughter.

The shoe was sealed in a transparent evidence bag. Mud from the Nile bank had dried on it, and fine sand had settled into one corner of the seal. It had come from a riverboat that was supposed to sink. After the revision, the boat reached shore safely, and the child who had lost the shoe went home in another pair. This shoe, left over from the discarded history, had nowhere to be returned to. The evidence room assigned it a number as usual and filed it away. Vika was carrying it past the staff lounge when he realized the birthday party was already over. Streamers drooped from the wall. Several balloons drifted under the ceiling, one of them slowly losing air and obscuring the last word of the banner that read HAPPY EIGHTH BIRTHDAY.

Sami was folding up the tables. When he saw Vika, he looked first at the evidence bag, then at the cake box.

"Couldn't you have come back one day earlier?"

"Do you mean yesterday here at the Station, or yesterday in Egypt?"

"You know which day I mean. Where's the cake?"

Only then did Vika remember the box in his right hand. He had smelled the cloying cream before he even reached the Station. One corner of the cardboard had collapsed, smearing frosting across the inside of the clear lid.

"You can visit a time before Khufu was born," Sami said, "but you can't make it back to watch my girl blow out her candles."

"You make it sound as though you made it."

"I did. She blew them out twice. The first time you weren't here, and she wasn't satisfied."

"And the second?"

"The lights went out halfway through."

Vika laughed. Every cable at the Station seemed to know Sami personally, yet on his daughter's birthday the lights had refused to listen to him.

The Temporal Bureau's Station belonged to no era in history, but it was subject to time all the same. Every clock there ran in the same direction. Staff could travel to yesterday, tomorrow, or a thousand years ago; the one place they could never revisit was the Station's previous meal. It was like a small terminal dispatching countless trains every day: the trains could go anywhere, but the platform itself had only one track.

They unfolded two chairs and shared the cake with the crushed edge. Sami balanced a leaking paper plate on one work glove. His daughter, Xing, was asleep. He said he did not want to wake her, then immediately opened his communicator and showed Vika twelve photographs. In the first eleven, Xing wore a paper crown and tried very hard not to smile. In the last, the crown sat on a hound's head.

"Whose dog is that?" Vika asked.

"A friend's. Nearly ate half the cake."

"Not a fussy eater, then."

Sami closed the lid. The index finger of his work glove had worn thin, revealing a sliver of skin dusted with insulating powder.

"I've got a bypass line to inspect today. Could you keep Xing's present for me? I bought her a mechanical watch. Promised I'd bring it home before school lets out tomorrow."

"A watch at eight?"

"She is always asking what time I'll be home. I tell her, when the hand reaches here. At least a thing like this won't suddenly send her a message saying RECOMMENDED DELAY."

"Then don't let her see the maintenance roster. She'll think every promise comes with an extension request."

Sami laughed so hard he pushed his chair back half a foot. He handed Vika the watch box, whose lid bore a tiny constellation, and went back on duty.

The next day's assignment was waiting on Vika's desk. In 1986, a materials researcher was preparing to leave her team to care for her father. It was a tiny retreat, but its consequences rolled all the way to 2008: an artificial-intelligence system intended for medical research would reach clinical use seventeen years late, and countless patients would never live to see the treatments that followed.

Vika did not need to invent the algorithm or carry technology back from the future. He needed only to ensure that an application for home-care support, mistakenly filed under Transportation Allowance, reached the correct reviewer in time.

Director Feng Cen showed him a photograph. A little girl in a red coat sat on a step, looking down as she wrestled with an over-tight knot in her shoelace.

"And after the revision?" Vika asked.

"You may go and see her."

Feng Cen's window showed a sky that was always overcast. The first time Vika visited, he had wanted to ask whether it ever rained there. Later he learned that it was the sky on the day Feng Cen's daughter was born. In the unrevised history, she lived for only eleven days. After the change, she lived to seventy-four, married once, acquired an obstinate disposition, and spent her final years feeding birds in a park where feeding was expressly forbidden. Feng Cen never introduced himself to her. He had seen even that park only once, and from a distance.

Vika put away the assignment card. Before leaving, he placed the watch box in his desk drawer and, when he remembered, sent Sami a message: **I've got it.**

Sami answered almost at once: **Don't open it.**

Then another message: **Especially you. Don't open it.**

Vika sent back a smiling face. As he closed the drawer, he thought, Tomorrow. I will definitely hand it over tomorrow. The Bureau could schedule innumerable missions to the second. Vika had simply never thought to put the delivery of a gift to a friend on his calendar.

## Two · The People Who Left the Mistakes In

The researcher in 1986 did not question the application that had suddenly been found. At first she assumed the review office had finally matched the applicant's name. Then she decided the whole thing had gone suspiciously smoothly. She ran down the corridor making calls, stepped on her own scarf, and had to come back looking for it.

Vika was supposed to leave at once, but he waited outside her laboratory until evening. He watched her slip her father's medical file into a document bag, take a notebook grey with pencil dust from a drawer, and hurriedly add one line of equations. The fluorescent lights in the corridor flickered. No one had time to report them.

An argument carried from the other end of the lab. Someone urged her to stay and promised the system could arrange a carer. She said she had tried; her father refused to let a stranger into the house. The other person said that if she left over this, no one would be able to interpret six months of project data. She said she knew. That was why she was hesitating.

The assignment sheet in Vika's hand did not instruct him to make the decision for her. Once the support was approved, she had both possibilities again: stay or leave. From the doorway, he watched her hold the telephone to her ear and explain to her father that she could arrive a little later the next day. Her father evidently spoke at length. While listening, she hooked the fallen scarf back toward herself with the toe of her shoe. At last she smiled and said, You don't have to worry about my experiment. I am not the only clever person there.

It is a shame this sort of thing never makes the revision report, Vika thought. The report contained one causal node. It did not even contain her scarf.

In 2008, the hospital walls were painted pale green, and free coffee stood in the waiting area for patients' families. The girl from the photograph was now a rehabilitation therapist. She crouched to tighten the straps on another child's brace. Vika recognized the broad eyebrows above the red coat. She did not recognize him. He watched for several minutes, bought an unimpressive cup of coffee at reception, and checked COMPLETE on the mission report.

His calendar was full through the following month: investigate a freighter about to capsize, correct a feed formula, prevent a misjudgment in a border town from escalating into armed conflict. The Bureau never published an annual number of lives saved, because in the new year many of the saved no longer matched the names in the old records. The institution preferred to speak of "improved indicators." That way no one had to decide how much a person who had never existed was worth.

As Vika prepared to leave, a woman came rushing down the stairs with a cardboard box. The bottom split. Exercise books slid one after another onto the floor. Vika reached down on instinct. One book fell open to dense calculations, half of them crossed out. Across the top someone had written: **THIS MUST NEVER BE MEASURED THIS WAY.** Half a page later, another hand had added: **NOT NECESSARILY.**

"What sort of class is this? How to contradict your own teacher?" Vika asked.

"They aren't mine." The woman did not look up. "Move, please. There is another one under your foot."

He lifted the box. She asked him to put it on a van already filled with packing crates from new equipment. The hospital had cleared two rooms on that floor for rehabilitation services.

"What were the rooms before?"

"A place where students learned to take things apart."

"They can learn from a system."

"Yes. Patients will wait three months less." She placed the final notebook in the box. "Which is why no one wants to keep these."

"You do?"

"Right now I want to get this box onto the van. My arms are about to fall off."

The woman's name was Noyi. She did not say where she had come from, and she did not ask Vika's name. He noticed several pale scars on her forearms, as though fine wire had cut them long ago.

At the van, she pulled one exercise book from the box and held it out to him.

"If you decide it is useless, you can give it back."

"If I travel with this many schoolbooks, people will think I have come to read their water meter."

"What do you do, then?"

"I inspect records. Mine just happen to span several centuries."

Noyi smiled. It was the first time they properly saw one another's faces.

He kept the exercise book in his luggage. A few days later, he discovered that it contained no secret theory. One answer was simply wrong. The system found the error in half a second and explained it with perfect clarity. Vika read the explanation and realized he still had no idea why the student had begun by measuring the height of the table legs.

He asked the system.

"One possibility is that the tabletop was not level," it replied.

"Are you sure?"

"There are at least four reasonable explanations. Given the available evidence, the first is most probable."

"Did the student ever find out?"

"The surviving records do not say."

Vika closed the book. In nine years at the Bureau, this was the first question he had encountered for which there was no future record waiting to be consulted.

He showed the notebook to a senior researcher. After one glance, the man said, "If you want to know what the child was thinking, the only reliable method is to ask."

"I don't even know the child's name."

"Then you don't know." The researcher handed it back. "Learning that is useful too."

Vika found the answer evasive. Yet whenever the system later offered three equally plausible explanations, he remembered the researcher's expression. It had not been smug. It had looked more like the face of a man who had finally put down something heavy.

## Three · The Sun Will Not Wait

The second time Vika met Noyi, in Jaipur, she was wearing only one shoe.

It was an eighteenth-century afternoon. The sun had heated the great stone astronomical instruments until they burned to the touch. Noyi crouched on a slope, stretching a fine thread. Her other shoe rested on the step below. Someone reached to return it. She shouted, "Don't move that yet!" and startled the young man so badly that he threw the shoe toward Vika.

Vika caught it. The leather smelled faintly damp.

"What exactly are you doing?" he asked.

"Waiting for the shadow."

"There is always a shadow."

"Not when you are standing on it."

Vika saw that his shadow fell directly across the scale and retreated into the shade of a tree.

The young man was called Arun. He was an apprentice at the observatory and repaired some of the smaller instruments used for practice. He was about to send a set of measurement records, heavily marked with errors, to another city. The Bureau had calculated that the resulting disputes would delay an irrigation project, preventing relief grain from reaching a famine in time. Vika's task was to replace the records with a verified copy whose conclusions were entirely correct.

Arun spent a long time comparing the replacement with his own notebook.

"You have made it so clear I won't have to come back tomorrow," he said.

"Isn't that good?"

"Of course. I can sleep late."

Noyi said, "Don't you still have half an instrument to repair?"

"You already know how unfortunate my life is."

He turned to the first page. "Why did you remove the trial where the wooden frame warped?"

"It was repaired," Vika said.

"And the next time it warps?"

"Someone will find it again."

"The next person who makes the same mistake could at least begin with this page. They would not have to spend another day finding the cause."

Vika was unconvinced. He had seen bad measurements kill people and did not consider being scolded a serious consequence. Noyi suggested that Arun repeat the measurements on site the following day. Vika agreed because Bureau rules allowed alternatives that achieved the same risk target.

By nightfall, they discovered there were no rooms left nearby. Arun said they could sleep at the observatory if his guests did not mind sharing the floor with stacks of records. He found three mats. Only two were intact. Noyi claimed one of the good ones, leaving Vika on a mat whose edges curled upward. In the middle of the night a cat emerged from nowhere, stepped on Vika's head, walked around it once as though deeply dissatisfied, and finally settled at Arun's feet.

Arun was awake under the lamp, redrawing the day's faulty curve. Vika told him to wait until morning. Arun said he could not sleep. His father had taught him to read the stars. The first thing his father taught him was not the names of the stars, but: if you cannot understand it, measure it again. Later his father died, leaving marks in his notebooks that Arun had never understood.

"Did you ask anyone?" Noyi said.

"My teacher. He said perhaps my father had calculated wrongly. Or perhaps his ruler was inaccurate." Arun set down the pen. "I am not afraid he was wrong. I just don't want to carry something into the grave when another person might have answered it."

Looking at the old paper in the lamplight, Vika suddenly thought of all the records he had classified as UNNECESSARY TO RETAIN simply because he could not explain them. He said nothing.

They made another mistake during the repeat measurement. The very first mark failed to align. After wrestling with the instrument, Arun discovered that the previous night's rain had swollen the mortise joint holding the wooden frame. He said he should have left a gap in the joint. Noyi reminded him that the night before he had confidently declared it would not rain. Arun protested that if the observatory could build instruments this large, it might first invent one that could predict the weather.

The old surveyor waiting beside them finally asked everyone to be quiet so he could hear the reading.

By evening, their measurements were close to the clean replacement copy. Arun placed that copy beside his old notebook. He sent neither away. Instead, he transcribed a new copy for the project manager.

"Your book tells them what the result ought to look like," he said. "Mine tells them why the frame can warp."

"They may not want to read the second one," Vika said.

"That is their choice. At least they will know there is another book."

The project was completed on schedule several weeks later. Vika requested that the field notes and failed pages be retained together. Feng Cen approved the request with a single annotation: **FEASIBLE.**

On the sandy road to the station, Noyi asked why Vika had not simply swapped in the replacement notebook.

"You said the patients waited three months less," he replied. "This time, too, someone will go less hungry. I only wanted to know whether there was another way."

"Did you handle missions this way before?"

"Before, I always thought there was no reason to spend an extra day."

"Do you have time now?"

"No. So don't drag me off to watch another sunset."

She did. Vendors nearby were packing their goods; travelers still hurried toward the city gate. The shadow crossed the stone steps by degrees, and the observatory keeper urged them to leave. Noyi stood on a slab they had just measured and seemed unable to make herself go.

"What year are you from?" Vika asked.

She crouched to lace the shoe that had nearly been lost.

"Much later."

"How much later?"

"An age in which none of your route tables ever truly stopped."

## Four · The Words Not Yet Printed

The rain in London was too fine for the eaves to catch. It was as though the whole city were trying to slip water down the collars of its pedestrians.

The printing press was heavier than Vika had imagined. He braced one corner and squeezed it through the back door. A strip of paint came off the wooden frame. The thin old man in front turned and delivered a long stream of abuse; the printer, Ada, caught only a few words but answered him without hesitation.

"Is he always like this?" Vika asked, panting.

Noyi said, "He used to think the entire world was obliged to move aside for him."

The old man had once been emperor of the Qin dynasty. Now he was standing in a cramped London print shop, arguing with workers because a machine would not pass through a doorframe. The Temporal Bureau had taken him from near the end of his life and treated his illness. He disliked hearing his old title used to his face and disliked being treated as a museum relic even more.

In more than a decade in London, he had learned to pay rent, compensate people for broken windows, and explain to people far younger than himself why last month's money could not be spent again this month. Some thought he was a political fraud from China. Others thought he understood power. The second opinion pleased him most.

"Have you come to make other people's decisions for them again?" the old man asked Vika.

"I came to inspect a register."

"Then you are late. There is more than one."

One register mapped the exchange of weapons. Another listed the rooms dockworkers had found for refugee families. Both were in the hands of the same network. To prevent a civil war, the Bureau wanted Vika to sever the network as a whole. The old man insisted that some contacts must be preserved, yet he also wanted to withhold a warning about danger at the harbor in exchange for one more day of negotiation.

"Do the people at the harbor know that someone has wagered their day for them?" Vika asked.

"If they know, they will panic."

"So you decide for them?"

The old man reached for his tea. Ada moved the cup aside before it could leave a ring on the proof sheets.

"I know what you are about to say," he replied. "But you have never seen how many die when a negotiation is almost complete and then collapses."

"You let me print the first page." Ada held up two sheets of newspaper. "I write about you on the second, and suddenly I cannot print?"

"The second page is wrong."

"You paid me to move the press, not to buy my editorial judgment."

The old man fell silent. His fingers tightened around the cup. He pointed at Ada and said that in his age a person like her would already have—then stopped. Someone knocked at the window: the boy delivering type, his arms trembling from the cold rain.

The room now contained four incompatible positions. Bureau rules required Vika to confiscate both registers. Noyi wanted to preserve the relief network. Ada wanted to print the harbor warning. The old man wanted everyone to wait until tomorrow. No answer could satisfy them all. In the end, Vika cut out the addresses of the refugee families and gave them to Ada for safekeeping. He copied the confirmed arms transactions separately and sent them to the Bureau. The remaining connections could not be verified.

The report should have read CLEANUP INCOMPLETE. Instead, he wrote RISK NETWORK DISMANTLED. It would not survive an audit. Only after signing did he notice the nib had left a blot beneath the last letter.

Ada's paper appeared the next day. She printed the harbor warning on the less conspicuous third page and left a narrow column in which the old man could publish his objection. He refused to sign it, saying the column looked like a shop advertisement. Ada replied that it was the best treatment she could offer an emperor who did not pay printing tax.

The warning did cause people to evacuate the harbor in haste. It also destroyed a negotiation already under way. In the records of the following weeks, Vika saw two families lose their livelihoods and another group escape before the harbor was sealed. Some wrote to thank Ada. Others threatened to burn her press. The old man brought the letters to Vika, waved one of them in his face as proof of recklessness, then shoved two others into Vika's hands and told him to read them himself.

"You see? Everyone can choose a page that proves them right," the old man said.

Vika wanted to tell him that at least they had not held the entire harbor's day hostage. The words reached his mouth but did not come out. Ada was arguing with a supplier over a paper debt. The old man had already gone to help her bargain.

The rain stopped briefly. Noyi washed ink at the back door. They crouched side by side and changed the water three times. The last basin was still grey.

"You know his real name?" Vika asked.

"Yes."

"You aren't going to tell me?"

"It is in your archives. He doesn't like it."

"How long have you known him?"

"Long enough. He once helped me borrow a transit permit."

Vika lifted his hands out of the water.

"Borrow?"

"I still haven't returned it."

At last she looked him directly in the eyes. Rain fell from the eaves into the basin between them, breaking the black surface apart little by little.

"You already knew who I was," Vika said.

"Yes."

"What else do you know?"

"I did not come looking for you because you are kinder than other people." She said, "I read your name in an old mission file. Sometimes you leave behind what you were supposed to erase."

"That doesn't sound much like praise."

"But I had no other reason."

Ada leaned out of the window. "Save your private business for after work. There are two more stacks of paper to bring inside."

Noyi stood. Vika was half a beat slower, so Ada left the heaviest box for him.

## Five · The Third Pipe

No one at the Temporal Bureau liked Feng Cen's office. Not because he was severe, but because the cloudy sky beyond his window seemed never to change seasons. Mira once secretly replaced it with a tropical beach. Feng Cen returned without comment and worked for half an hour before remembering that the view in front of him was now an ocean.

"Your London report does not match the field record," he told Vika.

Vika had come to submit a supplement. Noyi waited outside, ready to be taken into custody. She had used old routes at the Station to enter several eras, bypassing the Bureau's authorization system more than once. She had turned over her credentials and routes to investigators and said she possessed records from a future the Bureau had not preserved. She was willing to have them examined.

"People like this usually claim to come from a more distant future," Feng Cen said. "If she produces a photograph of herself at seventy, will you believe that too?"

"I didn't say I believed her."

"You lied in London."

"That has nothing to do with—"

"It does. Do not invent a reason for yourself merely to prove that you still know how to speak for someone else."

Feng Cen put the supplemental report back on the desk. He did not detain Noyi at once. Two departments happened to be short of staff: one was auditing bias in historical observations, and the other was restoring a retired teaching laboratory. Her knowledge of old routes might be useful. He granted her a temporary credential, provided Vika accompanied her and operations staff signed off on any use of equipment.

When Noyi received the credential, she asked whether she might first see the laboratory.

"You may visit the laboratory," Mira said. "Do not touch anything marked with red tape."

"What does red mean?"

"It means the other side belongs to operations. Do not cross the line."

They converted a disused laboratory between Research and Maintenance into a classroom. A ten-year-old timetable remained on the wall. The final entry read INDEPENDENT EXPERIMENT, followed by a row of blue postponement notices. The system could simulate any change in fluid behavior and explain it precisely, without wasting a word. But allowing a student to measure temperature with their own hands required eight forms. Vika completed three of them incorrectly. After reading them, Noyi said he must once have been ideally suited to the Bureau.

"I never used to make mistakes on applications."

"And you made three here?"

"Before, I only had to fill in the mission outcome."

Eleven students came in the first week. Two were taken home by parents who decided they were too young. One boy wanted only to know whether the system would mark a teacher's mistakes beside its answers. Another girl insisted on putting her paper airplane inside the incubator, claiming she was studying wind. Noyi told her to leave it outside. The child asked, "If it is an experiment, why can't it go in?"

"Because someone needs the cultures in there tomorrow. The paper airplane has its own bench," Noyi said.

At first she was poor at controlling a classroom. On the second day, three children of different ages interrupted her within ten minutes of an explanation of pressure differences. Finally she handed the chalk to the student who asked the most questions and told him to teach. Halfway through, the boy realized he was leading the entire class toward a wrong answer.

"What do I do?" he asked.

"Start again from here," Noyi said.

"That is too slow."

"You can look at the system's answer instead."

The boy chewed the end of the chalk, considered this, and erased the first two lines he had written.

The students wanted to try a real heat cycle. The retired rig had once calibrated the Station's heat exchangers. Its main pump had long been shut down, and heating power on the teaching side was limited. Yet its internal exchange chamber might still be connected through a maintenance bypass to the Station's high-temperature primary loop. Mira required physical confirmation that every heat-dissipation branch behind the wall had been isolated. Operations had issued only conditional trial permission: before a live start, the isolation had to be verified by the people on duty. The old rig's activation procedure checked only whether a confirmation form carried all required signatures. It did not confirm that those signatures belonged to the personnel currently responsible for verification. That defect in the procedure would later enter the accident report alongside Vika's false confirmation. Operations staff were occupied by a major overhaul. The earliest they could inspect this room was sixteen days away. Sixteen days later, part of the floor was due to be handed to the rehabilitation center. Appointments for treatment and rehabilitation were already booked through the next month. No one dared tell patients that expansion should wait because a group of children wanted to measure a heat change with their own hands.

When Sami came to collect the watch, he stood beside Vika's desk for five minutes. He had assumed the birthday present had long since been forgotten at some station. Seeing the unopened box made him almost embarrassed.

"I thought you would have opened it," Sami said.

"I was afraid your daughter would sue me when she grew up."

"Then I'll wear it for two days first, make sure it doesn't stop on the way."

He took out the watch, set it, and fastened it around his wrist. A spare strap remained in the box. Vika said he would give it to Sami next time, then put it in the drawer. Those were the last words they exchanged about the watch.

Sami helped draw two alternative experiments. One used cups of water. The other used a difference in liquid levels and required no heat source. Vika saw that the second was elegant. Noyi liked it too. But they had prepared for the heated trial for five weeks. The students had moved from drawing it on paper to covering the wall. One boy had guarded his strangely undulating curve so jealously that he had even skipped an online game with his friends that weekend.

Vika did not want to admit that he too wished to see what the curve would do. In the application, he classified this curiosity under EDUCATIONAL OBJECTIVE.

Sami arrived with his maintenance case to inspect the setup. He was still testing Xing's watch on his wrist, lifting it to his eyes every few steps.

"How much time have you borrowed from her?" Vika asked.

"Better than borrowing money."

Sami felt the left-side valves on the small loop one by one. His hand reached behind the frame and stopped beside an old label.

"How long has this bypass been out of service?"

"The drawing marks it as decommissioned," Vika said.

"I asked how long."

"There will be a record in the old forms."

"I can read a drawing. A drawing cannot inspect the site for us. If you want to run tomorrow's trial, the duty team must trace every connection in person."

"Can't you verify it?"

"Tomorrow I am repairing the main pump in the rear zone. I cannot be in both places."

Having said this, he unwrapped a sweet and put it in his mouth. Xing had chosen that flavor. He complained it was too sour, grimaced, then took a second one.

"Run one test without heat first," he said before leaving. "You always want to finish everything at once. Begin with the part that is easiest to inspect."

Noyi said, "Who told them to make the other part look so interesting?"

"You are allowed to find it interesting," Sami replied. "You still trace the connections one by one."

## Six · You Signed It

Mira did not come.

Her deputy sent a message: an old line in the rear zone was under emergency repair, and duty staff had to confirm the physical connections before they could rely on any marking. The system displayed a yellow warning on the electronic permit: **FIELD ISOLATION STATUS UNVERIFIED. POSTPONEMENT RECOMMENDED.**

Vika read it twice. Noyi stood beside him and did not hurry him.

That afternoon they dismantled the rig as far as they could. Two valves were closed. The pressure gauge read zero. Even if hot water carried away some energy, it should not enter a public area. Vika ran the numbers three times. The system judged the risk to be below the required threshold **provided the submitted connection assumptions were true**. The assumption was that the old exchange chamber's inlet to the primary loop had been physically disconnected, so the small amount of heat injected on the teaching side would circulate only in the sealed local loop. If the decommissioned bypass remained connected to the Station's primary loop, however, the trial pulse might trigger pressure compensation in an old interlock, drawing high-pressure hot fluid into a maintenance branch they could not see.

The system had not proved that the first configuration was real. It had proved only that a low-power trial would not harm anyone if the first configuration was real. They could not reach the final section of pipe. The people on duty could not come.

"Then we don't do it," Noyi said.

"We return the room tomorrow."

"We can apply for another cycle."

"We already did. You know the answer."

"I do. Which is exactly why we should stop."

Vika said nothing. He had just read the hospital waiting list. Those patients did not have time to spare either. He began to calculate: if this experiment succeeded, the students might learn to recognize an anomaly for themselves. If they saw only the system's curve, this classroom would soon become a storage room. As for the risk, he could count two closed valves with his own eyes.

He reduced the heat input to one quarter of the original plan and changed it to a short pulse. After checking the calculations repeatedly, Noyi agreed to record the trial. **Agreeing to reduced trial conditions** and **agreeing that isolation was complete** were two different things. When they filled in the final permit, however, they stopped distinguishing between them.

Vika signed the field-confirmation box.

Noyi looked at the name. "If Mira sees this confirmation, she will order us to stop."

"If the trial goes smoothly, the worst she will do is return the report."

"That is a very bad reason."

"I know."

Noyi turned the verification form around, found the yellow notice, and drew a heavy circle around it in the margin of her own notebook. She did not sign the isolation confirmation, but neither did she go to Operations and cancel the permit. Vika would revisit that small silence countless times. It left no written record, but he remembered clearly that each of them had wanted the other to stop what they were doing.

He knew he had no better reason. He still did not withdraw the application.

The next day Sami came to retrieve gloves he had left in the classroom. He and his partner, Andre, were preparing to inspect a pump bearing in the rear zone. Seeing the children lined up, he raised one hand like a regular entering a familiar restaurant.

"You are really doing it?"

"Low load. Isolation confirmed," Vika said.

Sami glanced at his signature.

"Someone traced the rear section too?"

Vika said, "The verification form has been signed."

Sami asked no more. He pushed the bag of sour sweets into his coveralls. Before entering the maintenance zone, he removed the watch and locked it in his locker. On the way out he reminded the youngest child not to press her nose against the clear cover. She replied that she was not a fish. Sami said at least a fish knew where the water was.

For the first five minutes of the trial, everything went well. The system explained the curve in real time. The students laid the line on the screen over the lines on their paper. One suddenly shouted that half of his prediction was right.

Vika was answering him when one sensor began returning to baseline more slowly than expected. At first the deviation was small enough to miss. The system recommended a stop. Noyi reached for the button while Vika continued looking at the numbers. The last ordinary sound he remembered was the child saying, "But my other half wasn't wrong either."

The heat source stopped.

More than ten seconds later, a dull crash came from inside the rear wall. It was not an explosion. It sounded more like someone knocking over an entire rack of empty steel drums. Several short, hard impacts followed. Vika ran to the door. The moment he touched the handle, steam burst through the gap.

"Evacuate immediately," the system warned.

Noyi seized his collar and dragged him back behind the safety line. The maintenance zone lay beyond that door. Sami and Andre were working there that day. There should have been no exchange of heat between them and the teaching rig. Vika could not understand how the pipe had suddenly come alive. From the instantaneous pressure curve, the system had already reconstructed a likely path: beyond the two valves, a third bypass temporarily restored during maintenance still connected to the Station's high-temperature primary loop. The old interlock had initiated pressure compensation after the trial pulse. High-pressure hot fluid had traveled through the unverified branch into the maintenance zone and burst open a joint that was being dismantled. The label had not been updated. The handover had not been signed.

A pipe he had never seen.

Andre's communicator opened. He shouted once. What followed was breathing and the scrape of metal. Mira cut the main pump from the control room. A second isolation valve closed, and red lamps lit the maintenance corridor all the way to the distant transit hall. The true shutdown was complete, but residual pressure in the primary loop still had to bleed slowly through the relief circuit. There was no way to recall the first discharge of superheated fluid.

Medics carried out two stretchers. Andre was moaning, one side of his protective suit fused to his injured arm. A white emergency blanket covered Sami's stretcher. For one moment Vika thought the name on it must be wrong. He stepped forward, opened his mouth, and made no sound.

The back of Noyi's hand was burned. Bright blisters rose on her skin. She stood there until Doctor Luo came past with a trolley and told her to move. Only then did she sit down.

That night's investigation found the yellow warning preserved in full. Mira produced the handover record and pointed out that duty staff were required to verify the bypass on site. Vika's signature was clean. So was its timestamp. The attachments preserved the numbers, photographs, and pressure readings from both valves. They lacked the one fact that mattered most: the actual connection in the rear zone.

Sami's watch was still running. A worker removed it from his locker and placed it in an evidence bag labeled with his employee number. The hour hand stood a little past nine.

Vika recognized the watch box. Suddenly he remembered the other strap Xing had once wanted, still forgotten in his drawer.

## Seven · Nine Seventeen

The property room would not let Vika enter. The attendant said no bag could be opened before the family arrived.

He sat outside. Noyi sat one empty chair away. Neither spoke. Andre was still in treatment. The director had suspended every teaching experiment, and an independent accident team had entered the Station. Vika and Noyi were not even permitted to explain directly to the family what had happened.

Vika looked at the clock at the end of the corridor. Its display changed from 9:16 to 9:17. He did not remember the precise time of the accident. He remembered sitting there and watching the clock move forward one division at a time.

"Can we go back to the day before?" he asked.

Noyi did not answer. Feng Cen was approaching from the other end of the corridor and heard him.

"Which day before do you mean?"

"Before he went inside."

"He lived at this Station for eleven years."

The Temporal Bureau could revise external history, but it could not return to the Station's yesterday. Even if they altered an event around Sami's birth, the people who remained at the Station would not vanish from its present roster. Versions of history could reorder themselves. The lives of people removed from their native era were lived only once, here.

"You used to say the rule was fair," Feng Cen said.

"I only ever thought about what it preserved for other people."

"You did not know everyone affected by your revisions then, either."

Vika looked up, ready with a retort, and saw that Feng Cen had aged enough for his shoulders to slope. The director did not pursue the question. He told Vika to arrange temporary housing for the evacuated children. With the main pump shut down, the entire Station had lost hot water that night. Many residents sat in temporary quarters wearing their coats.

Laila arrived with Xing to claim Sami's belongings. The child had not cried yet. Her school uniform was slightly too large, and each cuff had been rolled twice. Laila read one page of the inventory and asked, "May we take the watch?"

The attendant said yes.

"He bought it for his daughter."

Xing reached for it, then hid her hand inside her sleeve. Laila put the watch in the small box. Below it lay the badly worn work gloves, a set of keys, and a bag of excessively sour sweets. She closed her fingers around the keys. Outside the corridor, a truck reversed and sounded two warning notes. Xing turned at once, as though someone had called her from beyond the door.

Laila looked too.

"Who signed?" she asked.

Vika stood.

"I did."

She did not shout or even raise her voice. "Did Sami ask you before he went in?"

"Yes."

"What did you tell him?"

"I said the isolation had been confirmed."

She studied him. "I understand. Please do not ever use those words to explain this to me again."

For the following months, Vika and Noyi lost all operational authorization. The investigation found that outdated maintenance labels, a failed departmental handover, and unauthorized confirmation by the two experiment leaders had jointly caused the accident. Individual staff were suspended. Vika and Noyi bore direct responsibility. The loss, the compensation, Andre's long treatment and assessment of his ability to work all entered separate files. None would end with a letter of apology.

An independent safety commission and judicial oversight office outside the Bureau also joined the investigation. They retained the complete raw record. No one could reduce the responsibility to "the system failed to warn us" or "we did it for science."

Vika wrote to Laila. He stopped after the third letter. Her lawyer returned a single sheet: Transfer compensation to the agreed account. Do not enclose further correspondence.

Andre refused to let either of them take part in his care. Noyi asked whether she could repair an appliance for his family. The box was returned unopened, with one corner of the address label torn away.

In autumn, Vika and Noyi happened to sit at the same table in the canteen. He stirred a bowl of cooling soup. After watching him for a long time, Noyi said she wanted to open another classroom.

"You can," he replied. "I signed that form. We do not have the right to reopen as though nothing happened."

"I didn't ask for your permission."

He put down his spoon. They had their first real argument and left no room for either of them to look dignified. Vika said she merely wanted to prove she had been right all along. Noyi said he was now too afraid even to admit that he still wanted to teach.

"Go and open it," Vika said. "Just don't make me responsible for signing the safety permit."

She left, pushing her chair back too loudly. A little later, Vika noticed her scarf still hanging over it. He kept it beside him until the canteen was about to close, then took it to lost property.

Years later, Xing told him that for a time she had hated the watch. Its second hand kept moving forward, but her father would never open the front door again. She buried it under a stack of books for years. One day she took it apart to see what made it move with such determination.

She took it apart so thoroughly that it no longer moved properly at all.

## Eight · The Postwoman Without a Map

Noyi came looking for Vika a second time one year and eight months after the accident.

He was clearing a retired records room. Cabinets that had once held failed experiment logs had become a temporary pharmacy. Vika's job was to find obsolete catalogues and make room for medicine shelves. It required no permission to operate equipment. At worst, some clerical work might have to be done twice. Even so, he once reversed the order of the labels and spent two afternoons checking them again.

When Noyi called from the doorway, he failed to recognize her for a moment. Her hair was shorter. She carried an overfilled canvas bag, and half the handle of a folding umbrella protruded from a side pocket.

"Do you still have my scarf?" she asked.

"Lost property."

"They said it passed the holding period."

"Then there is nothing I can do."

"I did not come about the scarf."

"Why did you ask about it first?"

"Because I did not know what to say first."

Vika set down the catalogue in his hands.

She had not brought a complete plan for saving the future. The canvas bag contained transit timetables from different eras, a roll of obsolete interface drawings, and several family envelopes printed with unfamiliar place names. Those places lay in a future interval the Bureau's route map could not locate. The Bureau had always called it the Unstable Access Region. It did not claim that no one lived there.

Noyi came from there. Its residents called their period the Hidden Era. Some had once worked as observers for the Temporal Bureau. When they discovered that historical revisions repeatedly affected their families and friends, they built a network to block unauthorized access. It could not free them from cause and effect. It could only obstruct certain direct interventions. If the Bureau altered a birth in a much earlier century, people in the Hidden Era might still wake to discover a different neighbor next door. Only observers who preserved differences between histories would remember the former name.

"You came here to destroy the Bureau?" Vika asked.

"Not at first. I wanted it to stop certain classes of revision."

"At first?"

"I considered a faster method."

"Such as?"

"Find an operative they could not easily replace and make sure he never completed another mission."

Vika looked at her. She said it evenly, as though discussing something once considered in earnest and no longer possible to describe casually.

"Why didn't you?"

"I realized the organization would replace him immediately. I also realized I was not nearly as certain it was right as I had imagined."

She took an unsent letter from the bag. Its address was a breakfast shop. The ordinary glue on its flap had long since lost its hold.

"Later my mother told me not to treat another person as a line to be moved. I could not hear her then."

"What is she like?"

"She often forgets the kitchen timer and still makes excellent soup. She still lives there."

Vika did not touch the letter.

Independent archivists examined the material for three weeks. Han Ning, the reviewer, demanded a chain of custody for every original and grew increasingly impatient. The first group of files lacked checksums. The technical interfaces in the second group did not match their supposed dates. The third group, however, bore signatures left by observers who had disappeared in that era. He pointed to the passenger list of an interstellar resettlement project and asked why one person had two different sets of parents.

"Because that history was revised," Noyi said.

"If every inconsistency can be explained as a revision, your claim can never be tested."

Han Ning opened a comparison report from several years before. An earlier record said that a primary school had forty-two pupils; a newer local archive listed forty. The two rolls overlapped heavily. Two people were missing, and three family addresses had changed. In isolation, this could easily be a clerical error. Only if many records controlled by independent custodians showed the same replacement at the same time could that explanation gradually be excluded.

"Your isolation equipment preserves fragments of an older record," he said. "It is not an insurance policy guaranteeing that every person in the old version still exists."

Noyi nodded. When she first reached the Station, she too had mistaken such records for genuine protection. Then one day her mother returned from the corner and said the breakfast shop had a new owner. She knew the shop had always been there but could not remember the previous person. The isolation archive held a video of her arguing with the old owner over the price of milk. Noyi showed it to her. Her mother laughed and said, So I used to interfere in everyone's business then too. That evening, however, she did not ask the person's name.

After that Noyi seldom showed her mother differences between histories. She had learned that a record might remain while the lost person could not be recovered from it.

"That is why I agreed to let you inspect the originals," she told Han Ning.

He glared at her for a moment and turned the file back to its first page.

Feng Cen ultimately permitted one controlled verification visit. Vika would travel as a witness to earlier missions, still without operational authority. Before departure, Mira handed him their supplies: shoe covers, interface seals, and an incongruous hand-cranked lamp.

"To stop people stealing it?"

"If the automatic lights fail, at least you will have one method that does not require a network."

"There is no electricity?"

"Who knows. Without electricity, you will at least be able to see your own hand."

They passed through an extremely narrow access point and arrived at an ordinary apartment building. The lift was broken. A repair notice hung in the stairwell. Noyi saw it and visibly relaxed.

"Why are you pleased?" Han Ning asked.

"It was like that the last time I came home."

"You consider a broken lift good news?"

"At least the stairs are still in the same place."

Noyi's mother opened the door holding a knife and half an onion. She saw her daughter, looked at her hands, then at the two strange men.

"Why did you come on a Wednesday again?" her mother said. "They shut off the water for maintenance on Wednesdays."

"Was it Wednesday every time?" Noyi asked.

"Last time was Thursday. And you claim to have a good memory."

Noyi arranged her shoes carefully at the door, moving more slowly than usual. Only then did Vika understand that for him this was an unseen future, but for her it was a street from which she had been lost for years. She knew a pair of slippers was missing from the shoe cabinet but not whether her mother had given them away. She knew the lift used to stop once on the seventh floor but not whether the old man who always complained about it still lived there.

Han Ning did not hurry inside. He photographed the floor marker three times and stored each image in a separate isolated record. One corner showed the edge of an older number beneath the paint. He asked who had changed it.

"An earlier revision altered the construction contract for this district," Noyi said. "There used to be two buildings here. Now there is one. The fire-isolation layer in the stairwell can retain a limited old record. It cannot preserve everyone's memory."

"So if your mother was never born in the replacement history—"

"A photograph in an isolation layer would not give birth to her again," Noyi said.

Only after speaking did she notice that her mother had been waiting in the doorway throughout. She moved her luggage aside and let Vika enter first.

"You said four people."

"The other one went to inspect the interface."

"I cooked for four."

"I can eat the extra share," Vika said.

Her mother pushed the cutting board toward him. "In that case, would you finish this half of the onion?"

Lunch was not elaborate. Her mother's hands trembled. The vegetables came out in pieces of different sizes. The household system demonstrated a safe grip on the knife. She copied it and still cut unevenly. When Noyi tried to help, her mother turned the bowl around and said food did not have to be evenly cut to be eaten.

After lunch, Noyi took out the investigation plan and tried to use her mother's rehabilitation as evidence for why people should preserve certain everyday skills. Her mother read half of it and laid the document face down.

"Did you ask my permission before putting my life in your materials?"

"I didn't use your name."

"That is not what I asked."

Noyi lowered her head and slowly removed the page. Vika pretended to look for a glass and went into the kitchen. From there, he heard her mother ask, "Didn't you come home to eat?"

Noyi said, "Yes."

"Then could you spend today simply having a meal with me?"

That afternoon, Han Ning labeled every access record but did not activate the indoor audio or video devices. As they left, Noyi's mother packed them a jar of pickles and warned them not to let it leak into anyone else's luggage. Han Ning said its weight violated document-transport regulations. In the end, he carried it the entire way.

## Nine · The Return

An enormous route map covered a wall of the Return Museum.

At first Vika thought an artist had drawn the lines. They were irregular and did not all point toward Earth. Some crossed between stars. Others curved around great blank regions. Han Ning checked the plaque and found that the map was very old—made before humanity completed its first interstellar voyage.

The museum was quiet. Several children argued over the pilot's seat inside a landing-capsule model while their grandmother repeatedly told them not to pull that lever. Noyi bought three tickets. The machine failed to print the third, and Han Ning insisted on a receipt. They spent ten minutes at the counter paying for a route that no longer existed.

"The first group?" Vika stood before a photograph.

People in the image held flowers and waited for a descending spacecraft. A section of hull plating had been scorched black. The crowd looked as though it were welcoming home a championship team.

"The first migrants capable of establishing a permanent settlement beside another star," Noyi said.

"They came back."

"Yes. Everyone returned safely. At the time, that truly was worth celebrating."

Han Ning would not accept her narration. He wanted original acknowledgements, flight logs, contact protocols. Noyi opened the reading room and let him retrieve them himself.

The spacecraft had reached a star system already inhabited by another civilization. Its people did not open fire. They sent negotiators. Their routes did not cover every planet, but they had cities, place names, and intricate relations of rights and obligations. Humanity's first map marked some areas SUITABLE FOR DEVELOPMENT. On the other side's map, those places contained public cemeteries, migration grounds, religious sanctuaries, and several seasonal trade routes.

Earth proposed shared settlement. It also proposed purchasing land and leasing supply ports. Compromise was not impossible. Yet every plan that permitted settlement required the human outpost to negotiate directly with local residents instead of waiting for Earth or the Temporal Bureau to approve every final response to a crisis.

"They wanted independence," Han Ning said.

"Some did. Some merely wanted to wait two years less," Noyi replied.

The acknowledgements contained many statements of that kind: We are willing to take responsibility for maintenance here. We request a trial period of one winter. If the water supply fails, permit us to modify it locally. The children here begin school next year. They cannot wait for your next coordination session.

In the same archive, Vika saw the other side. The Temporal Bureau had revised the causes of an interstellar transport accident and saved more than three hundred people who would have suffocated in a gas leak. It had stopped an uncontrolled experiment in autonomy and prevented two cities from fighting over reserve supplies. The saved were not numbers. They went on to have families and jobs. Some even wrote letters complaining about the water company's fees.

Vika suddenly thought of Sami and no longer wanted to read. Noyi did not press him.

The following day, the three traveled to an old archive station on an outer settlement called Talo. The city had been evacuated. One archive technician remained on duty. His name was Luen. He was very short and wore a wool waistcoat completely unsuited to the climate. His first words were: "Coffee goes on the table to the right. The other surface has already absorbed two generations of coffee stains."

Talo had not disappeared in a single disaster. It had once been a promising settlement, with its own storm-observation group, repair workshops, and forty-seven technicians trained through local experiments. Advanced artificial-intelligence research systems handled much of medicine, transport, and ecological maintenance. This did not prevent people from doing good work.

Then the machines found more reliable predictive methods. Budgets moved to new automated modules. The storm group began reading central reports instead of making observations. The city really did lose fewer people as a result. Old equipment became children's toys. Students learned to understand the new conclusions but encountered less and less of the work required to measure again when a prediction failed.

Luen opened a complaint filed thirty-seven years earlier by a young engineer. She had discovered that a local storm would alter the intake of a cooling loop. The system's standard solution depended on parts that had not yet arrived. She asked for space where several apprentices could test a crude local method.

Luen enlarged the letter. A faint scratch crossed the signature box. The writer's name was Zoya. She had not used the results template then in fashion. Her opening sentence read: If you permit me to use Workshop Six for two weeks, we may complete one local test before the final transport departs. She attached seventeen wind-speed and temperature readings taken by hand. Question marks stood beside several.

Her application requested only twenty working days and two apprentices able to operate the old rig. The approval system had calculated that the old equipment was expensive to maintain and would not predict as accurately as the central model. It also provided a thoughtful recommendation listing the research she might accomplish in three years if she transferred to the central team. The final item even included positions for which she was well suited. She printed the recommendation and wrote across the first page: I know. But Workshop Six is here.

Luen opened an audio recording. A motor spun uselessly amid heavy background noise. Then a girl asked, "Why do we have to know the temperature inside the pipe? We aren't planning to live here forever."

"You don't have to want to know," Zoya said. "But you just adjusted the valve. Tell me why you turned it that way."

"The system said it would stabilize."

"It was right. I asked why."

The recording went quiet. Someone laughed. Someone else suggested eating first. Zoya laughed too. "We can eat first. When we come back, we begin with that flow diagram."

It was not a heroic speech. The next twenty minutes contained metal banging, interruptions, and an argument about who had taken the screwdriver. Vika listened to the end. He wanted to know what happened to the two apprentices. Luen told him to continue through the file.

The central office sent a courteous reply: on the basis of system-wide costs, continued use of the verified alternative was more prudent. If the local research had merit, participation in the next joint program was recommended.

"What happened?" Vika asked.

"She tried it herself," Luen said. "She connected a discarded copper vessel, old tubing, and two condemned sensors. She built a temperature instrument that worked only in Talo's climate. People laughed at her for wasting time. She stayed in the repair room until the hot plate burned two holes through the soles of her shoes, and she still refused to stop."

"Did it work?"

"The trial achieved part of what she expected. Before that winter ended, the evacuation order came."

"The system's forecast was right?"

"Yes. The next storm was far worse than they expected. If people had stayed, they would have died. There is nothing to argue about there."

Luen showed them the instrument: an ugly copper shell inside a clear case. Beside it were the engineer's name and two dates—birth and departure. She later worked at the central research station, did very well, and won awards. Luen had read her reports when he was young. One passage on Talo's winds was still used.

"Then at least she had the chance to continue her research," Vika said.

"At Central she did things a hundred times more sophisticated. But Talo never produced another group willing to spend three years learning the wind. Eventually there was not even an institution willing to pay for such a group."

One apprentice followed Zoya to Central and quickly became an excellent maintenance engineer. The other stayed on Talo and moved into transport scheduling, earning considerably more than before. Before the evacuation they had argued over whether their modest savings should be spent on new sensors. In the end, they bought nothing because the hospital needed a replacement medicine refrigerator.

"Why do you remember it so clearly?" Han Ning asked.

"The one who stayed is my sister," Luen said. "Whenever she talks about Zoya, she curses her for refusing to leave when it was time. If you asked now, she would also say that what she learned in that workshop was more useful than many training courses."

"Where is she now?" Vika asked.

"Two days away. Two grandchildren, very busy. I keep thinking of calling her and asking whether she remembers the copper pipe they cut too short. Then I decide she probably does not want to tell the story again."

Luen closed the recording. Part of him was still in that old meeting and still believed purchasing the modules had been the reasonable choice. Vika watched him and realized that a person's regret and a person's mistake were not so easily separated.

Luen wiped the display case. "Don't look at me. I voted in favor as well. My nephew needed an operation that year. Without the new module, there was no bed."

He did not repent his choice. He showed them the council minutes and emphasized that the costs had been real and the benefits had been real. He wanted someone to remember only that another capacity—one that had not yet had time to yield a benefit—had also truly existed.

That night, Noyi took Vika through Talo's old research station. Many rooms had become storage. One still contained ten tables and a whiteboard covered in scratches. Vika asked why Zoya had not given her method to the system for preservation.

"She did. The system even improved it," Noyi said.

"Then what exactly was lost?"

Noyi pointed at a half-erased string of dates on the board. "There used to be a field-review meeting here every week. Apprentices brought disputed readings. Engineers argued in front of this board until late at night. Later the meeting was cancelled. The experimental procedure remained in the system. But when the next unforeseen phenomenon appeared, no one knew who should object, who should organize another measurement, or who was responsible for challenging the central judgment."

Vika saw an ugly smiling face drawn on the board. Beside it someone had written: **STILL DIDN'T WORK TODAY. BACK TOMORROW.**

It had been a long time since he visited a laboratory where someone wrote SEE YOU TOMORROW on the wall.

## Ten · There Is a Barbershop in the Last City

When they went to inspect the last cities of the future, Han Ning insisted on bringing a second recording system. Noyi complained about the weight. He said she had layered three interpretations over her evidence last time, and he genuinely could not tell which layer stood independently.

"If you really distrust me, why come?" Noyi asked.

"Because your conclusion deserves doubt, but your evidence deserves examination."

They checked population registers, then food production, urban maintenance, and outer-settlement migration. The final decline in population did not happen in one year or follow one kind of disaster. Many people lived to old age. The sick received careful treatment. The last maintenance workers replaced filters on schedule. Births grew rarer, and the inhabited areas people could maintain together repeatedly contracted. No policy meeting ever announced that humanity was going extinct.

Han Ning found records that did not fit Noyi's explanation. Some settlements with independent technicians had evacuated all the same. In one major ecological accident, the automated forecast had not failed; the supply system had genuinely exhausted its reserves. He wrote down each inconsistency. Noyi did not interfere.

The final city stood beside the sea. Most buildings retained their walls. Trees along the street had grown crooked through gaps in the architecture. In a small square, Vika found a barbershop. An appointment card still hung on the door: Wednesday afternoon, two customers. A thin coat of dust covered the chairs. The mirrors were intact. He pushed the door open. Wind lifted hair from the floor, and the fine strands floated in the light for a long time.

Behind the shop was a small kitchen with half a packet of biscuits. Someone had scrawled a complaint across the back: **SWEETER EVERY YEAR.** The writing was crooked, as though an adult had deliberately copied a child's hand.

Several appointment cards remained beneath the counter. Fewer people came for haircuts in the final years, yet the corners of each card were neatly trimmed. Vika turned one over: Thursday, four o'clock. A-Wan. Shorter, but not too short. Beneath it someone had added: She said I took off too much last time.

He tried the storeroom cabinet. The door stuck. Afraid of breaking it, he let go. A yellowing hand-drawn map was pasted to its side. Three nearby streets curled across it in thin lines, and an arrow pointed to a bakery still open. It even showed the steps and advised wheelchair users to approach from the other end.

This little shop had remained for many years after the last birth record they had verified. Vika thought that for many people the end of the world might not be the day the final person died. It might begin when a familiar shop no longer opened. He wanted to take the map. In the end, he photographed it instead.

Han Ning went outside to check the public-service terminal on the corner. It started at once and asked whether he wished to restore the streetlights. He said yes. At dusk, the whole street lit all the way to the sea. Vehicles moved slowly past according to their programs. No one came to a window to look at the suddenly illuminated road.

Vika sat on a chair outside the barbershop that had waited there for years and remembered the photograph of the paper crown on the dog.

"Are you certain this is the last city?" he asked.

"According to the records we have verified, yes," Han Ning replied.

"That doesn't prove shutting down the Bureau would prevent this."

"No."

"It doesn't prove every revision they made was wrong."

"No."

Han Ning placed the two recording systems side by side on the step. "I can confirm that people once lived here and cannot now be found. As to why: if we write the conclusion too fully, someone will use it to decide for the next generation."

Noyi stood beside the sea with her back to them. For the first time Vika thought there was nothing mysterious about her at all. She was simply terrified—terrified enough to spend years collecting records, terrified enough to say things that had not yet been proved so that someone would leave with her sooner.

He went to her and asked whether her mother would leave her old street.

"No."

"You didn't try to persuade her?"

"I did. We nearly stopped speaking."

"What happened?"

"She asked me to bring her the salt and stop blocking the kitchen door."

Noyi crouched to pick up a small stone. The wind smelled of salt. Suddenly she lowered her head. Vika wanted to hold her. His hand rose halfway and stopped. She waited a moment, then leaned into him herself. They did not discuss the report they would take back until every streetlight had come on.

Before returning to the Station, Han Ning completed his certification: **IN THE VERIFIED VERSION OF HISTORY, HUMAN POPULATION REGISTERS TERMINATE. FIELD OBSERVATION FOUND NO SURVIVING COMMUNITY. THE CAUSE OF EXTINCTION CANNOT BE ATTRIBUTED TO ANY SINGLE POLICY OR ACCIDENT.**

He signed very slowly. Noyi stood beside him and did not ask him to change a word.

## Eleven · The Dissenting Vote

Feng Cen watched the footage of the Last City three times. On the third viewing, he noticed the appointment card on the barbershop door and asked them to pause.

"My daughter hated anyone touching her hair," he said.

No one replied. Members of the operations committee expected an obscure pronouncement. Instead, he switched on the lights and asked Mira to arrange a station-wide risk review of the maintenance systems.

The following day, a notice headed PROPOSAL TO SUSPEND TEMPORAL REVISION appeared outside the canteen. Someone mistook it for a holiday schedule and asked how many days the suspension would last. Someone else asked whether commuter services would keep their normal times. A nurse from the clinic added a line at the bottom: **ROUTINE PRESCRIPTION RENEWALS WILL CONTINUE.**

The first public meeting lasted six hours. The microphones failed three times. Feng Cen argued that knowledge of the Last City made it more urgent to improve historical revision, assigning greater weight to distant autonomy and long-term scientific capability. Mira replied that this would still allow a present institution to decide which capabilities deserved protection and when they might be withdrawn. Some agreed. Some objected. Most wanted to know whether the transit station would open the next morning.

In the fourth hour, a woman born at the Station stood and demanded that everyone planning to leave first surrender their family medical files. Her son was alive because of an early revision and was now studying at a university in another historical period. She did not care whether the future produced more storm researchers. She did not want to hear that shutting down the Station would not automatically undo an existing revision. She wanted to know whether, if someone altered that revision again, she would still recognize her son.

Mira explained for a long time. The woman finally sat, still shaking her head. At the end of the meeting, she held the door for an old man in a wheelchair. Vika heard her say, "Whatever the vote tomorrow, the pharmacy opens at nine." The old man replied, "I have worked there for twenty years. It will not stay closed merely because they all became philosophers overnight."

Elsewhere, people posted handsome leaflets calling for an end to all revision because the future must never again be manipulated. The next day, a patient receiving continuing care after temporal surgery tore one down and said he did not wish to become anyone's slogan. The young man who had posted it apologized and collected the remaining sheets one by one. Watching from a distance, Vika thought these particular inconveniences were harder to manage than any speech declaring the correct position.

Laila sat in the back row. Xing had grown taller and dozed against her shoulder. Someone invoked Sami's accident as proof that crucial decisions were safer in the hands of a system. Someone else said it proved everyone must learn to verify equipment.

Laila raised her hand.

"Do not turn Sami into evidence for either side," she said.

The chair asked her to explain. She held a creased sheet listing four matters: Xing's school for the following year, treatment funding for the long-term injured, resettlement for Station employees who left their jobs, and an address where every bereaved family could later find the responsible institution.

"Continue the argument as long as you like," she said. "But Xing starts school on time next term."

Feng Cen asked whether she had anything else. She did: no prize was ever to be named after her husband. She did not want her daughter to attend an award ceremony one day and be asked to smile while thanking the accident.

Noyi sat in a middle row. The scar on the back of her hand still tightened when the weather changed. She did not raise her hand. Neither did Vika. Since the investigation, neither had voting rights, and no one wanted to describe institutional transition as a great meaning they had found for the accident.

Before the second meeting, Vika was asked to list the facilities that could sustain ordinary life independently after the temporal channels closed.

The committee commissioned a separate causal assessment of what shutdown would actually mean. Mira invited three researchers who opposed retirement, and Han Ning audited their data. The conclusion was neither romantic nor despairing. Ending new revisions would not rewind existing medical, educational, and rescue interventions one by one. People alive in the present history would not vanish the moment the doors closed. But once the old network stopped observing across eras, future risk information could no longer reach the Station as it once had. Some residents would also lose their only route home.

The final page listed what could not be solved. People born only in the very distant future depended on innumerable events that had not yet occurred. Closing the network changed the conditions under which those events would happen. No one could guarantee that the era in which Noyi's mother lived would still arrive in its present form. Mira refused to let the researchers remove the sentence. People were going to use this report to decide where to live, she said; the thing they least could afford to omit was what no one knew.

Vika put that report first among the documents to be disclosed to every household. Feng Cen initially objected. They argued until they discovered that the dispute was not over facts, but over how frightened the same paragraph would make the residents.

Vika ran daily between warehouses and clinics. Water, power, air circulation, and spatial transport all had independent systems; closing the temporal gate would not end civilization at the Station. Yet medicine supply and the identity records of residents from several unusual eras still depended on the Bureau's old network.

He brought Feng Cen a supply list as tangled as a spiderweb. The director put on his old glasses and studied it.

"You used to hate forms," he said.

"Now someone gets medicine because of this one."

"That was also the purpose of the forms we completed all these years."

"I know."

Feng Cen pointed to an extremely expensive medicine for a rare disease. If they transferred it in the proposed manner, dozens of patients might be unable to pay within three years.

"Then we secure the supply before discussing closure," Vika said.

Feng Cen removed his glasses and looked at him.

"You aren't going to debate me?"

"I will. Before that, I want to know these people have somewhere to go."

They worked together for a week, temporarily waived several transport fees, and used institutional assets to establish an independent medicine trust. The money quickly proved insufficient. Mira proposed cancelling a historical-observation project with no obvious current purpose. Operations agreed. Feng Cen reminded them that the project might be monitoring a catastrophe that had not yet occurred.

"How much are we supposed to preserve?" Mira asked.

"I am not certain," Feng Cen said.

It was the first time Vika had heard him say those words in a meeting.

In the end, the Temporal Bureau suspended new large-scale historical revisions. Existing rescue and transport operations would undergo individual review. Permanent retirement still faced fierce opposition. Reopening broad revisions required signatures from the actual channel operators. Mira refused to sign. Her successor was equally unwilling to take responsibility while the evidence gap remained.

Not everyone was persuaded. A young operator slapped his resignation on the table. He did not want to spend his life in ethical hearings about how to open and close a door. He wanted to draw a salary and raise his children. Mira accepted the letter and asked him to work his night shift first.

Feng Cen returned the final unsigned activation request. As Vika passed, he heard the director drop his pen into a drawer with a very soft sound.

## Twelve · Which Way of Saving

On the sixteenth day after the shutdown plan was announced, an alert entered the old temporal network's emergency queue.

A cooling failure was due in an outer settlement. In the original history, damage spread from several greenhouses into an entire residential district. The Bureau had already calculated a reliable solution: go back six months and cause the local procurement director to sign a different contract. The contract would save residents and property, but it would also divert funding from a newly founded independent materials laboratory that, ten years later, would otherwise train several excellent engineers.

Once, this would have been an entirely ordinary assignment. Feng Cen wanted it executed immediately.

"People will die," he told the committee, "when we already know how to save them."

Mira asked the system to calculate the procurement revision separately from emergency aid. It produced a narrower intervention: deliver evidence of the fault, repair advice, and medical supplies, then warn residents to evacuate. Most lives could be saved this way. Not all property could be protected, and there was no guarantee local people would believe the warning in time.

"Does the system oppose my plan?" Feng Cen asked.

"It has no preference between values," Mira said. "It supplied two estimates of consequences. Someone must be responsible for what remains."

He looked at Vika. Vika no longer had permission to operate. This time he could not even deliver a letter.

"Do you also think we should leave them there to wait for their own mistake?"

Vika had just returned from temporary housing for injured residents. Dust covered his soles. After a moment, he said, "Send whoever is qualified. I ask one thing only: do not write the rescue and a ten-year contract signed on their behalf on the same line."

The rescue team departed. Operations sent medical staff, technical advisers, and observers able to deliver the raw evidence. After verifying their identities, the local council opened evacuation routes. Some people refused to leave. An old man insisted on returning for his cat. A rescuer went with him, and both were injured. The cat crawled out of a vent by itself the next day and stared through the glass as if nothing had happened.

Far fewer lives were lost than in the original history. Material damage was greater than under the procurement revision. The independent laboratory survived, but that year it had to borrow a municipal warehouse. Vika later visited. The roof leaked, and three young researchers caught the water in plastic sheets. One asked whether the Bureau had sent him to take control again. He said no, he had merely brought a crate of inexpensive measuring tools.

They examined the equipment with great seriousness. The youngest opened every compartment and pointed out a missing probe. Vika said the packing sheet noted that the probe would arrive at the end of the month.

"Then why does the delivery form say COMPLETE SET?" she asked.

Vika could find no answer. He handed her the packing sheet and asked her to note the discrepancy in the acceptance record.

Feng Cen read the rescue report and congratulated no one. He knew another history could have saved more homes. Knowing that did not shorten the present list of injuries. He continued to believe that people in the future would regret rejecting a road known to be safer.

Vika did not celebrate either. Several children had minor injuries in the evacuation. Someone had almost died for a pet. The greenhouses would take half a year to repair. The SUCCESS box at the end of the report remained empty. Mira replaced it with several lines: who made the decision, who carried it out, who bore the loss, and which consequences remained unknowable.

At the end of summer, the transitional committee voted to retire the existing large-scale revision network while preserving the public archive and the possibility that future generations might study temporal technology again. More than twenty dissenting opinions were attached.

The committee's authority extended only to this Station's equipment and budget. It could not speak for every future resident or prohibit later people from researching time. Opponents observed that closing the network also affected many people not present in the room. Supporters replied that continuing to use the network to alter those people's histories had never obtained their consent either. Legal staff wrote no perfect judgment for either side. They produced the most concrete retirement agreement they could: existing residents would receive independent resettlement; licensed rescue missions would continue during the transition; the archive would be opened in full; any future restart could not reuse the present system of single-person authorization.

Reading the agreement, Vika remembered his name beneath the yellow warning. The lawyers had argued over that final provision for several nights. He had no standing to tell them it was safe enough. He still hoped the nights had not been wasted.

Feng Cen voted no. He organized no sabotage and did not pretend to change his convictions. He continued transferring his knowledge to the researchers who would follow.

Noyi stared at the voting notice in the long corridor.

"Aren't you happy?" Vika asked.

"I thought I would be happy when it stopped."

"Should we find something to eat?"

Noyi looked at him. "You are thinking about dinner now?"

"Otherwise I don't know what to do."

Together they put away the meeting chairs. One had a wheel jammed in the carpet. Noyi crouched. Vika said he would do it. They both seized the wheel and pulled it off entirely.

"We only had to put away one chair." Noyi looked at the wheel in her hands. "Now we need tools to put it back."

"This time we can consult the maintenance manual."

"I know."

They sat on the floor laughing. A moment later, Noyi remembered that her mother still lived on the other side, and the laughter stopped.

## Thirteen · The Last Departure

The notice announcing the Station's closure was revised three times. The first delay was for an old man's medicine. The second was for a missing cat someone insisted on finding. The third did not change the date; it merely replaced NO SERVICE AFTER DEADLINE with PLEASE CONTACT THE DUTY OFFICE.

People argued outside the warehouse every day. Some wanted to take cabinets assembled thirty years earlier. Others refused furniture but insisted on the painting from the kitchen. Noyi's mother decided to remain on her old street. Noyi tried several times to persuade her, spreading travel tickets and a medical plan across the table and accounting for everything.

"Will there be neighbors where you are going?" her mother asked.

"Yes."

"Do I know them?"

"No. You can get to know them slowly."

"But my neighbors are here. I have no wish to begin knowing everyone again."

Her mother packed the salted fish Noyi used to like into a lunch box and added several packets of medicine. Noyi said the medicine was available at the destination. Her mother replied that she knew, but the two of them always forgot to take it. Vika cut vegetables nearby and tried not to speak. He made pieces of different sizes. Noyi's mother watched for a long time, could bear it no longer, and took the knife from him. Her movements were much slower, yet the pieces came out more even.

Once, Noyi tried to arrange a cross-era medical agreement for her mother. The institution at the other end was patient and sent every required document. Its final question was: if local standards changed in the future, who would confirm that she continued to consent? The law had no simple answer. Noyi stared at the screen all night. The following day she called her mother and admitted that what she wanted to hear was simply: I will come with you.

Her mother said, "I know. But I want to live here."

After a pause, she added, "Aren't you always telling people that some problems have no answer good for everyone? Why, when it is our turn, will you not admit it?"

Noyi laughed once, but her eyes reddened. She said knowing and accepting were different things. Her mother said, Then eat first. Even across all these years, I still do not trust you to argue on an empty stomach.

On their final visit, her mother asked whether they had found a place to live.

"We are looking," Vika said.

"Don't choose a kitchen that is too small. When she is anxious, she stands in front of everyone."

"Am I really that bad?" Noyi asked.

"You are standing in front of me now."

On the day they left, her mother did not walk them downstairs. She had arranged to make dumplings with her neighbors for dinner and refused to cancel an ordinary promise for a farewell. Noyi closed the door. Two floors down, she stopped and wanted to go back and ask once more.

Vika waited at the foot of the stairs. He did not hurry her.

In the end, she did not return. They carried their lunch boxes for a long way. Only when Noyi found a pair of knitted socks inside—one cuff higher than the other—did she begin to cry beside the road.

Laila and Xing had already departed before the Station's last channel closed. Their compensation and care agreements had transferred to an independent trust jointly supervised by victim representatives and operating institutions. From a distance, Vika saw Xing holding her school bag and saying something to her mother. Laila laughed. They did not come to him. He did not approach them.

Feng Cen's final file was a theoretical manuscript on temporal intervention. He required that it be published in full, together with his dissent. Someone asked why knowledge capable of rebuilding the system should be preserved after shutdown.

"Burning a manuscript," Feng Cen said, "does not give the people who follow better judgment."

He did not leave. Until the final group of patients boarded, he continued checking names. Vika helped steady a wheelchair. Feng Cen inspected its restraint and asked a nurse to replace a loose strap. Years before, the two men had discussed a seventy-four-year life across the same desk. Now, for the sake of pushing one chair through a door, they argued at length over which side should turn first.

"There is still time," Feng Cen said.

Vika knew he did not mean the wheelchair.

"I don't know whether we are right."

"You didn't know last time either."

"Last time I signed my name and said I did."

Feng Cen let go of the chair. He stood for a moment and adjusted his old glasses. The nurse told them to clear the way. Both stepped back. Later, they left through different exits.

Mira walked at the end of the line. The routes connecting the Station to different eras were being dismantled one by one. The final channel led only to a fixed exit. It did not contain enough components or trained people to rebuild the network from the other side. Each time she passed through a door, Mira checked the number remaining in the empty Station. She had done this for decades; her fingers moved before her mind.

On the other side was an ordinary corridor in 2008. Recruitment advertisements and a notice for an apartment hung on the wall. A driver pushing a trolley asked them to clear the passage. Noyi stopped under the notice with two suitcases full of books and old possessions.

"There is a kitchen," she said.

"A window?"

"Yes. It looks quite large."

Vika saw the small print at the bottom: WINDOW FACES FIRE ESCAPE. He did not tell her immediately.

When the last door disappeared, nothing shook and no year suddenly became another. A delivery truck sounded its horn. Through the window, the driver motioned them away from the unloading area.

Noyi pushed one suitcase into Vika's hands.

"Come on. We should eat first."

He caught it too quickly. The bottom struck his knee and nearly knocked him off balance. Noyi laughed and turned to look for a restaurant already open.

## Fourteen · Ten Years Are Not Long Enough

During their first year outside, Vika was unemployed for three months. The new apartment did have a kitchen window, but it faced the neighboring building's fire escape. Every morning a cat from that building sat on the stairs and watched him make coffee. Once he forgot to close the window. The cat jumped inside, overturned an open sack of flour, and left more than a dozen floury paw prints across the counter. The building manager reminded them to close the window next time or incur a cleaning fee. Noyi thought they should first find the cat's owner. Before they could finish arguing, Vika had already picked up a cloth.

The Temporal Bureau's compensation obligations did not end with the institution. Vika found a low-paid job organizing equipment documentation. The system identified omissions; he went to the people who had actually dismantled the machines and checked them. Many older workers could no longer name the part numbers they had used, but remembered that "the machine made a noise every Friday and started working again if you tapped it twice." He entered such comments exactly. His manager said they were not sufficiently standardized. Vika suggested adding a standard field for nonstandard phenomena observed by actual maintenance staff.

Noyi began teaching experiments at an old community school. Almost every parent asked the same question during enrollment: if the advisory system knew everything, why should children soak their clothes, scorch paper, and cover a table with mess? At first she gave a long answer. Later she said only: attend one class; if it is dull, do not pay.

Fifteen students came in the first term. Nine remained in the second. Six new ones arrived in the third. Enrollment was never large. Sometimes there were not enough children to fill one laboratory table.

Once, the class used three cups of water for a temperature experiment. A girl called Lulu replaced what should have been cold water with warm water, changing every number. A boy called Qiao angrily demanded an apology. She said she merely wanted to know what happened to the curve if a condition changed. They argued until the lesson ended. The advisory system produced an exact prediction. Qiao refused to look at it and insisted on repeating the experiment himself.

That was the first day Vika helped in class. He dried the cups and labeled them one by one. In secret, Lulu wrote on the final cup: **THE ONE QIAO HATES MOST.** He pretended not to notice.

In the third year, the school failed to secure an expansion. The funding was needed for more beds at the rehabilitation center. The committee was courteous from beginning to end. Noyi put the rejection on the dining table and cooked as usual. After watching her chop vegetables for a long time, Vika asked why she had not fought the decision.

"I did. Their need really is more urgent."

"Do you think they are wrong?"

"No."

"Then why are you angry?"

"Because they have good reasons, and I still cannot bear to give up that classroom."

She set down the knife, opened her terminal, and wrote to the rehabilitation center asking whether it had any unused clear boxes. The next day it sent four old ones. A fine crack ran down the side of one, and a note was attached: **FOR WATER EXPERIMENTS ONLY. NOT FOR MEDICINE.** Noyi was so pleased she spun around holding the box and nearly collided with Vika.

In the fourth year, Vika entered a university replication challenge. His problem concerned heat propagation through a special material. The advisory system had proved it thoroughly decades before and had even organized the erroneous derivations into a textbook. The organizers supplied only the earliest observations and a set of instruments. Participants had limited time to retrace the search for an answer.

On the first day, Vika mounted the probe incorrectly. On the second, he proved a true and useless lemma. By the third, others had submitted their reports and he was still repeatedly measuring a thin strip whose edge cut his finger. The host asked whether personally repeating a problem long solved by the system made him feel discouraged.

"Yes," Vika said.

"What was most discouraging?"

Vika raised his index finger. A small adhesive bandage covered its tip. "I forgot my gloves while cutting the metal strip."

The host removed that answer from the final interview.

Noyi came from home and found his dinner cold. His paper was dense with circles and arrows. At the bottom he had written: **WHY MAY THIS BE TREATED AS ADIABATIC?**

"The instructions didn't say?" she asked.

"They did."

"Then believe them."

"I want to know under what conditions the assumption stops being true."

He did not win. The judges noted that his conclusion omitted a correction factor. A younger woman on his team did far better. Accepting the prize, she said she had wanted to prove her mother wrong because her mother always claimed she had no patience. Everyone laughed. Vika applauded from the audience. Afterward, he continued working on the metal strip for half a year until he understood the missing term. The system verified that he now understood and asked whether he wanted a results report generated.

He considered it and said no.

"There is no new discovery," he said.

"The process of understanding may be preserved as educational material," the system replied.

"I will think about it."

In the end, he bound several hundred pages of notes. On the cover he wrote: **SEVERAL STEPS NOT YET VERIFIED INDEPENDENTLY.**

In the sixth year, they moved to Mars. The temporal channels were gone, but spatial transit between planets continued. Mars had work and a school willing to accommodate small-scale experiments. They argued repeatedly about the house. Their worst argument began when Vika accepted a moving date selected by the system without asking. Noyi discovered that the date was her mother's birthday. She said nothing that evening, packed her possessions into boxes, and prepared to leave ahead of him.

Vika said they could change the tickets. Noyi asked whether he did not always believe the system's arrangement was best. He said yes, but this time it had not asked her.

She put down the box. The following day, they carefully compared the dates and found that the system's recommendation really was much cheaper. They took the cheaper service after all, but moved their departure time by two hours. Noyi cooked a meal her mother had loved and arranged the table as her mother did in an old photograph. Halfway through, she complained that the food was too salty. Vika asked whether her mother cooked like that too. Noyi said no, her mother was good at it.

In the eighth year, a research institution invited Noyi to submit long-term data on independent experimental education. The students had produced no discoveries. Few became scientists. Some did not finish the course. Several reviewers argued that a machine could provide equally accurate training in understanding, making expensive physical classes unnecessary. Another put the question more sharply: if the children would not obtain better careers from the course, why should anyone continue paying?

Noyi thought for a long time and did not write "civilizational resilience" in the application. She wrote that the children were already choosing to spend their own time and were willing to pay a small fee. The classroom used safe old equipment and did not displace public life-support needs. If people later judged the money badly spent, they could discuss it openly. As for whether learning to measure a water level again was worthwhile, she could not write the answer for every family.

She did not receive all the funding. In the end, she secured the room for only two afternoons a week.

In the ninth year, Lulu grew up and left Mars for a long-range engineering school. She sent a photograph and asked whether the three-cup experiment still existed.

The photograph came with a long letter. Lulu and her classmates were debating whether to use the system's recommended structure for a new hull material or first build a less efficient model that could be inspected by hand. She did not oppose the recommended design. She disliked that by the third round of discussion everyone had begun quoting the same summary. She put the model on the table and invited every critic to turn one screw.

Some changed their minds. Others became more firmly convinced that the system was right. Her professor wrote: Interesting experimental design, but do not mistake stubbornness for independent thought. At the end of the letter, Lulu said the professor was probably right—at least half right. Noyi showed Vika. He asked whether she remembered Lulu replacing the cold water with warm water. Noyi said she did. At least then, she said, Lulu would tell her classmates which condition she had changed.

They spent an evening on the reply and wrote less than one page. Vika insisted on deleting a sentence about "the scientific spirit." Noyi said it was a perfectly smooth sentence. Vika replied that its smoothness was exactly why it did not sound like anything they would say to Lulu.

Noyi dug through old things and found the cup marked THE ONE QIAO HATES MOST. A chip in its rim made it unsafe for water. Vika suggested throwing it away. She said they should keep it for now. Later, while they were organizing the kitchen, it broke by accident. They spent a long time cleaning up. Neither remained sad for very long.

In the spring of the tenth year, Xing knocked on their door.

She was in her early twenties, with short hair, carrying an old biscuit tin. Inside was a disassembled mechanical watch. Gears and screws were sorted into several paper packets. Vika opened the door wearing a badly washed apron and assumed she was making a delivery.

"Do you recognize me?" Xing asked.

Vika removed the apron and tried to find words he should have said years before. Xing spoke first.

"Do you charge for watch repair?"

"That depends on how badly it is broken."

"It was broken for a long time. Then I broke it again."

"That counts as two repairs."

Xing considered. "I also brought a school experiment. If we include that, can I have a discount?"

Vika could not help laughing. He stood aside to let her enter.

She placed the tin on the table. He remembered the blue box Sami had brought and the spare strap he had never passed on. Incredibly, it was still inside an old drawer. Vika took it out and placed it at the edge of the table.

Xing picked it up. She did not speak at once.

Noyi returned from the classroom next door and stood in the doorway. She asked whether anyone wanted lunch. Xing said it was three in the afternoon. Noyi replied that this was all the more reason to eat; Vika had been saying "in a moment" since noon.

Seven clear cups stood on the table. Xing took out a chaotic drawing of a water circuit and asked Vika to inspect it. He immediately saw two unreasonable connections and was about to correct them when he stopped and asked which section she planned to test first.

"This one." Xing pointed at an awkward elbow.

"Water will leak there."

"Then I'll put the catch basin underneath first."

He went to the kitchen for a basin. When he returned, Xing was looking down at the watch. She suddenly asked why her father had not come out that day.

Vika did not retrieve the 472-page accident report.

"I don't know," he said. "I know what he was doing. I do not know why he continued doing it. That day I told him the isolation was confirmed. I was wrong when I said it."

Xing nodded. She said it was all in the report; she had wanted to hear him say it. Vika asked when the watch had stopped.

"Last week. I broke it myself."

"Then why did it stop at nine seventeen?"

"It happened to be nine seventeen when I damaged it."

She looked at him. "Some people will see it and say this is the time left by my father's death. It has nothing to do with that day. I don't want every object to be about his death."

Vika was still holding the basin and did not know where to put it.

Xing said, "I want to repair it because he wore it. I want to wear it too. That is all the reason there is."

After a moment she asked whether they could do the experiment first and repair the watch once the water was ready. Vika agreed, provided they used gravity head only and did not connect a heater. Xing rolled her eyes and said her teacher had told her nine times.

"Ten," Noyi called from the kitchen. "One more will not hurt."

Xing said adults were annoying. Then she arranged the cups one by one and began pouring water.

## Epilogue · Home for Dinner

In 2018, winter came slowly to Gravel Bay City on Mars. Vika seldom looked at his old mission records. Sometimes he dreamed that Gate Three in the transit hall had lit again, passengers for the past and future waiting in line, while he could not remember which pipe he was supposed to inspect first.

Xing's experiment won second prize at her school's open day. First prize went to a nutrient-saving plant culture system designed by another student. Xing thought the judges biased and went home to write a long appeal. Then she discovered she had recorded the height of one set of cups incorrectly and deleted it. Vika listened to her complain by phone about her carelessness and did not rush to console her.

The mechanical watch was finally repaired. A slight mismatch remained in the replacement gears, and it lost two minutes by midnight. The system could provide calibration instructions. After reading them, Xing dismantled the watch twice more and reduced the loss to half a minute. She was pleased but worried her mother would be upset, so she decided not to mention the replaced parts yet.

Only seven students enrolled in Noyi's new term. One boy attended twice and stopped because he preferred remote lessons. Noyi did not press him. She had the remainder of his fee refunded. Another girl came every week. Although the system could explain every step perfectly, she always confused hot and cold water. Vika taught her to label the cups. She said labels made it less fun.

They had planned to visit the sea that weekend. Gravel Bay had an indoor body of water that simulated tides, which the tourist brochure insisted on calling an ocean. The water was very clean and deep enough only for standing play. After studying the ticket price, Noyi said she wanted to see the real thing.

"Why isn't an ocean on Mars real?" Vika asked.

"Tell me whether it has ever seen a real storm."

They bought two tickets to Rio. The Temporal Bureau's channels between eras had closed; spatial transit continued, and even people who had opposed the shutdown all their lives used it to travel. On Saturday morning, Vika ate breakfast on Mars, changed through two spatial gates, and stood in the sunlight of Earth before afternoon.

The wind on Sugarloaf Mountain was strong.

Before they went up, Noyi bought two bread rolls. Vika said food was sold at the summit. She replied that the summit sold it at summit prices. When they arrived, the wind inflated the paper bag, and a fearless seabird nearly stole one roll.

From the terrace outside the cable-car station, the boats on Guanabara Bay looked like small strokes of white paint. The houses of Urca lay between the mountain shadow and the shore. Vika stood at the railing for a long time. He had visited many cities. Some were later revised again. Some ceased to exist after a change in history. He had also seen a city where the appointment card still hung outside the barbershop and no one ever came for a haircut.

Noyi gave him the larger half of the bread.

"Are you thinking about that city again?" she asked.

"A little."

"Is there still an ocean there?"

"There was the last time we went."

She did not ask more. They ate against the railing. A cable car descended in the distance. A child crouched nearby, drawing it as a square box with several wheels underneath that did not belong there. The child's father tried to correct him. Before his hand reached the page, the child sent him away to hold down the corner of the paper instead. The man obeyed. When the wind rose again, he failed to hold it. The sheet turned over. The child angrily threw down his pencil. His father bent first to find it and received another scolding once he had.

Vika laughed. Noyi asked why. He said it was nothing; he had suddenly thought of Xing.

That morning Xing had sent a photograph of the mechanical watch. It was running, though it lost more than twenty seconds every twelve hours. She said she was working on it and would show them when it was fixed. Vika typed DON'T TAKE IT APART AGAIN, deleted it, and wrote: Next time, remember to photograph it before you begin.

It was still light when they descended. Noyi checked restaurants in Sydney and suggested dinner there.

In the cable car, a tourist insisted on extending his camera beside Vika's shoulder to film the bay. Vika moved aside. The tourist followed. After watching for a while, Noyi asked in an uncertain accent whether he would take their photograph. Delighted, the tourist positioned them beside the window and took six. In every one, the window frame cut away half the landscape. Noyi thanked him and kept the last. Vika was looking down at her. She alone faced the camera.

"Is it good?" Vika asked.

"At least it proves you left the house today."

"I thought photographs were chiefly for scenery."

"Scenery doesn't need photographs."

She laughed first and put the terminal in her pocket.

Vika checked the local time. "Will it still be dinner there?"

"If the restaurant is open, dinner still exists."

"Didn't we just eat?"

"That was an afternoon snack."

The spatial station downtown occupied an underground shopping center, with a souvenir shop beside its entrance. The gate read Vika's transit code, then refused to open because a metal buckle on his backpack jammed the inspection slot. An attendant crouched to loosen the strap and spent three full minutes on it. Vika would later say they crossed half the Earth in minutes and were delayed by an unremarkable buckle.

Outside the Sydney station, the sea wind came from another direction. Lights along Darling Harbour reflected in the water. Excursion boats turned one after another. The restaurant Noyi had chosen was not yet serving hot food. A waiter brought water and two menus and asked whether they wanted an appetizer. Looking at the menu, Vika thought of the distance from the mountain they had just left and, for once, did not want another number.

"How long did it take to fly from Rio to Sydney before?" he asked.

Noyi was uncertain and consulted her assistant. The system supplied flight and connection times, then recommended a nearby restaurant with better ratings and a shorter wait.

"Move?" Vika asked.

"Aren't you hungry?"

"I am."

"Then we wait here."

They waited another twenty minutes. People at the next table were arguing over an interstellar expedition, then over whether repairs to the old city's water circulation should take priority. One woman said the expedition had consumed the municipal budget. Another produced an itemized account showing the funds did not even belong to the same department. The argument did not end. The three began comparing reports, knocked over a small glass, and required the waiter to replace the tablecloth.

After listening for a while, Noyi pointed to a dish the system did not recommend.

Vika reminded her, "It says the salt and fat are both high."

"The menu says that. I know."

When it arrived, Noyi took one bite and slowly lowered her fork.

"That salty?"

"Would you like to try?"

Vika pushed his plate toward the middle, inviting her to eat his instead. She did not. She drank half a glass of water and took another bite. Later they packed half the dish. Vika could not tell whether she liked it or simply could not bear to waste the money.

By the time they returned to Mars, the residential district was in its night cycle. A child slept against the wall beside the pressure door while his father, carrying a toolbox in one hand, supported the child's shoulder with the other and waited for another lift. Beyond the corridor window lay dark red ground. A maintenance vehicle moved slowly beneath the lamps. The smell of a neighbor's bread drifted from the far end. They had eaten enough and became slightly hungry again.

Noyi went to the window and checked the potted plant. Automatic irrigation kept the soil at the correct moisture. She touched it anyway, got dust on one finger, and on her way to wash her hands put the Sydney container into the refrigerator.

Vika opened his terminal. A long-range research system had published a new materials structure that day. He understood only half the abstract and did not want to face it. Instead he opened his unfinished heat-transfer exercise. The system had already provided the answer. He remained stuck on the third step.

He asked the system to explain again from the beginning. Halfway through he called a stop, opened the notes he had bound the year before, and found a crooked diagram. At the point he had never understood, he had omitted a thin contact layer. He added it and continued, then got the sign wrong. The system pointed it out. He swore and began again.

Noyi took from the drawer a letter to her mother. The envelopes had no addresses and formed a thick stack. She added one line: Today we went to Rio, then Sydney. She wrote about the boats in the bay, the dish that was too salty, and the buckle on Vika's backpack jamming at the gate. She wrote the buckle twice. The first version read like a maintenance report, so she crossed it out and tried again.

The kitchen timer sounded. Noyi asked what they should eat the next day.

The system immediately proposed three menus: one optimized for nutrition, one for cost, and one for ingredients about to expire in the refrigerator.

Vika looked at the calculation he had just completed and asked whether she would eat noodles.

"Didn't we have bread today?"

"Bread and noodles are different."

"There you go again."

She folded the letter and went to the kitchen. Together they opened the refrigerator and saw the previous night's food and the half container from Sydney. Noyi pushed that container farther inside.

"Tomorrow," she said.

"What about tonight?"

"Wash the dishes first."

Vika looked once at the unfinished calculation on the table, put down his pen, and went to wash the dishes.
